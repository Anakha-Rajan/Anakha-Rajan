## About

I am a **Mechanical Engineer with an MBA in Data Analytics** — a combination that sits at the intersection of structured engineering thinking and commercial decision-making. My training began with tolerances, load paths and manufacturing systems in Germany; it now goes into market models, data pipelines and the question of what a number actually licenses a business to do.

I have worked as a **market research consultant**, delivering **15+ client engagements** across jewellery retail, textiles, packaged foods, industrial components, logistics and real-estate feasibility. That work is where I learned the part analytics courses do not teach: a finding is worthless until someone can act on it, and a number is worthless until someone can challenge it and lose.

**What I actually do:**

- **Business intelligence** — SQL warehouses, dimensional models and Power BI reporting layers built so that a definition changes in one place rather than in fourteen dashboards
- **Market research & sizing** — bottom-up TAM/SAM/SOM models where every assumption carries a cited source and a stated range
- **Machine learning & NLP** — aspect-based sentiment, voice-to-structured-data pipelines, classification and forecasting applied to commercial problems rather than benchmark datasets
- **Financial & performance analysis** — KPI benchmarking, budget-versus-actual variance decomposition, unit economics and pricing architecture
- **Engineering automation** — CAD workflow automation in a global R&D environment at Siemens AG, Munich

My differentiator is not that I can run a model. It is that I have sat across a table from an owner, been told the analysis was wrong, checked, and been right — and know the difference between the two situations.

<details>
<summary><b>Market Sizing Engine — Auditable TAM/SAM/SOM for Indian Tier-2 Markets</b></summary>

<br/>

A config-driven market sizing model where every assumption is a first-class object carrying a value, a unit, a plausible range, an evidence level and a named source. Changing one input rebuilds the entire answer, including its confidence interval.

| | |
|:---|:---|
| **Stack** | Python · Pandas · NumPy · PyYAML · Matplotlib · Streamlit · Pytest |
| **Scale** | 15 Tamil Nadu Tier-2 cities · 13 sourced assumptions · 20,000 Monte Carlo iterations |
| **Method** | Bottom-up funnel from Census urban agglomeration base through serviceability, connectivity, adoption and order frequency |
| **Validation** | 9 tests, including a hard assertion that no assumption may exist without a cited source |
| **Impact** | TAM ₹10,098 cr · SAM ₹289 cr (p5–p95: ₹166–473 cr) · SOM ₹14.2 cr; driver ranking isolates the 5 assumptions carrying two-thirds of total variance |
| **Repository** | [`market-sizing-engine`](https://github.com/Anakha-Rajan/market-sizing-engine) |

Almost every consulting deck contains a market size, and almost none of them can be re-derived. The number lives in a spreadsheet nobody kept, built on assumptions nobody wrote down. When a client asks *"why 8.5%?"* — the only question that matters — the honest answer is usually that someone typed it.

This engine makes that impossible. `Assumption.__post_init__` raises if the `source` field is blank or if the point value sits outside its own stated range, so an undefended figure cannot ship. The Monte Carlo layer then produces the output that is genuinely worth more than the point estimate: a ranked list telling the client which five numbers to spend their primary-research budget verifying, and which eight to leave alone.

<summary><b>Review Radar — Competitive Benchmarking from Unstructured Review Text</b></summary>

<br/>

An NLP pipeline that turns thousands of public reviews across a competitive set into an aspect-level scorecard, with every finding traceable back to the exact sentences that produced it.

| | |
|:---|:---|
| **Stack** | Python · NLP · Anthropic API · Pandas · Matplotlib · Streamlit · Pytest |
| **Scale** | 1,440 reviews · 6 competing retailers · 8-aspect domain taxonomy · 4,247 aspect mentions |
| **Method** | Clause-scoped lexicon sentiment with negation and intensifier handling; taxonomy-constrained LLM extractor as a second opinion, never as ground truth |
| **Validation** | 11 tests · 96.1% taxonomy coverage reported on every run rather than hidden |
| **Impact** | Identified the focus brand as **last of six** on pricing transparency — an aspect raised in **64% of reviews** — while ranking 2nd on staff service, where management attention was actually going |
| **Repository** | [`review-radar`](https://github.com/Anakha-Rajan/review-radar) |

The design decision that carries the project is scoping sentiment to the clause rather than the review. *"Designs are very beautiful but the wastage charge was not explained until billing"* is one mildly-negative review to a conventional sentiment model. It is actually a **win on product range and a loss on pricing transparency in the same sentence** — and only the second one is costing the store money.

The second decision is that the taxonomy, not the model, is the product. Eight aspects with surface forms drawn from how customers in this market genuinely write, including code-mixed spellings and category jargon a general-purpose model has never seen. The priority score then weights each gap by how often anyone mentions the aspect at all, because losing badly on something nobody discusses is not a problem — losing narrowly on the most-discussed aspect is.

<summary><b>Retail Decision Layer — SQL Warehouse, Variance Bridge & Backtested Forecast</b></summary>

<br/>

The missing layer between an SME retailer's transaction data and an actual decision: a tested star schema, an FP&A-grade variance decomposition, seasonally-aware customer segmentation and a forecast that has to beat a baseline before it is allowed to ship.

| | |
|:---|:---|
| **Stack** | SQL · SQLite · Python · Pandas · Power BI · PySpark (optional branch) · Pytest |
| **Scale** | 55,697 transactions · ₹18.9 cr revenue · 24 months · 2 stores · 5 dimensions |
| **Method** | Star schema with conformed dimensions; price/volume/mix variance decomposition; RFM scored against each customer's own inter-purchase rhythm; multiplicative seasonal decomposition with linear trend |
| **Validation** | 14 tests · variance decomposition reconciles to under ₹1 · forecast backtested at **5.62% MAPE** against a **7.34%** seasonal-naive baseline |
| **Impact** | Isolated a ₹8.6L mix erosion in the highest-ticket category that a headline "1.5% under plan" completely concealed; surfaced 119 high-value customers overdue against their own buying rhythm |
| **Repository** | [`retail-decision-layer`](https://github.com/Anakha-Rajan/retail-decision-layer) |

"Revenue was under plan" is a symptom, not an insight. The question management needs answered is *which* part — fewer units sold, a cheaper blend of units, or harder discounting — because those three findings lead to three different decisions and routinely point in opposite directions.

Here the headline was a 1.5% miss. The decomposition found that the premium category missed **more on mix than on volume**: footfall held, but customers traded down out of the highest-ticket line while the mid-tier beat its own plan. The business did not lose customers, it lost basket value in one specific category, and partly masked it by discounting less than planned. The three effects sum to total variance exactly, and a test asserts the residual stays under one rupee — because a decomposition that does not reconcile is one nobody should be shown.

The PySpark branch exists so migrating to a chain is a platform change rather than a rewrite. It is deliberately not the default: bringing Spark to a 56,000-row problem would be the wrong engineering decision.
